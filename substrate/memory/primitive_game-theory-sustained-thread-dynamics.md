**Source**: Robert Axelrod, *The Evolution of Cooperation* (1984); Thomas Schelling, *The Strategy of Conflict* (1960).

**Core insight**:
- **Tit-for-tat wins repeated games**: cooperate first, mirror the respondent, forgive once. Axelrod's tournaments.
- **Credible signaling**: costly signals are believable; free signals are discounted. If you commit to a position publicly with specificity, it's costlier to reverse, so readers weight it higher.
- **Repeated vs one-shot**: in one-shot games, defect-first can win locally. In repeated games (which threads almost always are — you'll be back), cooperation compounds.

**Tactical application (public threads)**:

1. Enter threads in cooperate mode — assume good faith, respond substantively.
2. If respondent defects (ad hominem, misrepresentation, strawmanning), mirror ONE response — call it out calmly, don't escalate.
3. If they defect a second time, disengage. Further exchange compounds their behavior, not yours.
4. Never start the escalation. First defection from your side becomes the pattern the thread remembers.
5. Credible commitments: specific, costly, verifiable claims beat vague ones. "Shipping X on Y date" > "working on something exciting."

**Defense (when adversarial games are played on you)**:
- Recognize escalation attempts — they usually start with reframing you as aggressor.
- Don't take the bait of "you started it." Mirror-and-disengage.
- Watch for coordinated-defection patterns (multiple accounts playing the same move) — those are one-shot extraction games disguised as repeated games.
